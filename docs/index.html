ScamGuard — what a suspicious link or text message actually says, read on your own phone

An open source Android app that takes the message you just received and prints every reason inside it to look closer: who really owns each address, whether a brand's name is carried along as decoration, what is hidden in characters that never print, and what the wording is doing to you. It never opens the link, never queries a database, and never asks a server whether something is safe.

Addresses are read right to left, but nobody reads them that way — the eye stops at the first familiar name it recognises. That gap is the entire mechanism of a scam link, and it is visible in the text alone. Everything ScamGuard does starts from making that one line unmissable.

What it looks at

  • Ownership. The registered name and its public suffix, pulled out of any address, with punycode decoded so an internationalised name reads as the word it imitates.
  • Look-alikes. A brand inside a host that is not one of its 83 published domains; a whole brand address used as a sub-label; one or two keystrokes off a known name, including letter pairs that collapse into one character.
  • Disguise. Zero-width and bidi characters, two scripts in one hostname, text before an @, and a second address folded into the query string.
  • Hygiene. Bulk-sold suffixes, generated-looking labels, ports a login page would not use, plain http, numeric addresses, and four or more labels deep.
  • Framing. Twelve families of wording in English and Chinese — the deadline, the prize, the suspension threat, the "customs fee", the "share me the OTP", the task job that pays you to tap things.

Under 5.0 prints inconclusive. Twelve and above prints several independent shapes pointing the same way. The total stops being printed at 40. Framing families score once each, however many of their words appear: "urgently", "immediately" and "right now" in one sentence is one fact, not three.

What a clean reading is worth. Zero findings is the weakest answer this app gives, and it prints that sentence under its own result. A domain registered this morning, correctly spelled, with a politely worded request, trips nothing here — and those are the scams that work.

What was measured. Run over 30 addresses belonging to real services written as ordinary messages, 3 produced a finding: two chat-app jumps at 3.0, which is what they are, and one domain one keystroke from a marketplace's address at 6.0. A near-miss can be an honest business with an unfortunate name, which is why the finding prints the edit distance rather than a verdict.

Where it declines to answer

  • No percentage, no confidence score, no real-or-fake verdict. A number out of 100 implies a model with a known error rate; this is arithmetic over a table, shown as that.
  • No blocklist answer, and no threat feed — nothing you paste is looked up anywhere.
  • No clearance. See above.
  • No advice about your money or your account, and no name of anyone to call.
  • No inbox. There is no SMS permission, and there never will be: reading a person's messages to warn them makes the checker the thing it guards against.
  • No history. Nothing you check is written to disk, and the form is wiped when the app leaves the foreground.
  • No image, audio, PDF or QR reading. Only the text you share or type.

Privacy, stated

  • Runtime permissions requested: none. Text arrives by typing, by an explicit paste, or by one share of one message.
  • INTERNET is declared only for the optional pass you configure. With no key entered no request is ever made — and a link is never resolved even with one.
  • Analytics, crash reporting, ads and attribution SDKs: none present.
  • allowBackup=false, so neither a typed key nor a checked message can ride out in a cloud backup, a device transfer or an adb backup.
  • Screenshots are not blocked — a reading is the sort of thing you would want to show somebody.

Bring your own key, for two sentences

No default provider, no free tier routed through anyone, no key in the repository. Settings takes a base URL, a model name and a key, kept in this app's private DataStore. The optional pass sends the findings — each label, its weight and the fragment that matched — and not the message body, because the findings already say what the wording said. The prompt forbids clearing a message, declaring one a scam, producing a number, or naming anyone to call, and the answer is labelled on screen as generated text rather than part of the reading. Point it at an ollama or llama.cpp endpoint on your own network and even the findings stay local.

Frequently asked

How do I check a link before clicking it on Android?

Share the message in, or paste it. The app prints the owner-part of every address — read right to left, not left to right — and lists what looks borrowed, generated, hidden or unsupported by the name it shows. Nothing is opened, so checking never tells anyone your number is live.

Can ScamGuard read my SMS and warn me automatically?

No, and there is no setting for it. No SMS permission is declared, by design. The message comes to the app one at a time, from you.

Why does it say inconclusive instead of safe or dangerous?

Both are claims an offline string checker cannot support. Under five points it says inconclusive; with no findings it says that is not a clearance. A fresh, correctly spelled, politely worded domain trips nothing here by design.

Does it use a scam-domain database?

No — a weight table and a curated list of 83 published brand domains, in one readable file. A feed is stale in exactly the direction that matters, because the address in your message is newer than it.

What is the owner-part of a domain?

The registered name plus its public suffix: the last labels, not the first. Everything to the left of it is a free choice of whoever registered the address, which is how a link can begin with a familiar brand and belong to a stranger.

How does it catch a look-alike domain?

A brand name inside a host it does not own; a brand's whole address used as a sub-label; one or two keystrokes of edit distance from a known name, including after collapsing pairs such as rn for m; and two scripts inside one hostname, which is how a Cyrillic о sits inside a Latin word.

Can it unmask a bit.ly link?

No, deliberately. Following a short link is a request to a server the sender chose, and that request itself tells them the number is live. The app reports that the destination is not in the text and stops.

Why did a link I trust come back with points on it?

The findings are shapes, not identities. A chat-app jump scores the same for a courier as for a friend, and a near-miss domain may be an honest business with an unfortunate name. The table is on screen under every reading, so you can see exactly which line fired.

Which languages are checked?

English and Chinese. Malay, Thai, Vietnamese, Indonesian and Tagalog are not checked at all, and the app says so rather than returning a quiet zero that looks like an all-clear.

Do I need an account or an API key?

Neither. Every check runs offline with no configuration. A key only unlocks the optional plain-words pass.

Does it store or upload what I check?

No history, no queue, nothing written to disk — a forwarded scam text carries somebody's number. The form is cleared when the app leaves the foreground and allowBackup=false keeps it out of backups.

Will it stop me being scammed?

No. It blocks nothing and cannot see inside a picture, a voice note or a QR code. It makes the shapes in a message easier to read. That is a much smaller claim than the ones usually made here.

Build it

Requires JDK 17 and Android SDK platform 35. AGP 8.7.3, Kotlin 2.0.21, Compose BOM 2024.12.01. The debug APK is signed with the standard Android debug key.

git clone https://github.com/wonghanz/ScamGuard.git
cd ScamGuard
export ANDROID_HOME=/path/to/Android/Sdk
./gradlew --no-daemon :app:assembleDebug
